## What is an e-CVT?

Few car parts are as badly named as Toyota's e-CVT. "CVT" makes most people picture a continuously variable transmission with a steel push-belt between two pulleys, which is what a [CVT](/glossary/cvt/) usually is. Mechanically, the e-CVT has nothing in common with that. It is a single planetary gearset, called the power-split device, linked to the gasoline engine and two electric motor-generators (MG1 and MG2). It has no belt, no pulleys, no shift mechanism and no fluid coupling.

The continuously variable behaviour comes from software controlling the two motor-generators. MG1 works mostly as a generator, soaking up engine torque and turning it into electricity. MG2 works mostly as a motor, driving the wheels. Changing MG1's speed lets the system set engine speed and wheel speed independently of each other, and that independence is what continuous variability means. The driver gets a CVT-like feel, with engine RPM that doesn't track road speed directly. The hardware doing it is a different machine from a belt-and-pulley CVT.

Most Toyota hybrids sold in Canada use the e-CVT. That includes the Prius, RAV4 Hybrid, Camry Hybrid, Corolla Hybrid, Highlander Hybrid, the Sienna (hybrid-only in the current generation) and most Lexus hybrids. The exceptions are the systems built around a stepped automatic, such as Lexus's Multi Stage Hybrid in the LC 500h and LS 500h and Toyota's i-FORCE MAX trucks.

## Why it matters in Canada

Toyota's e-CVT has shown some of the best transmission durability of any vehicle sold in Canada. Taxi and ride-hail drivers put very high mileage on Camry Hybrids and Priuses, and the e-CVT rarely turns out to be the part that ends their service life. The reason is how little there is to go wrong. No belt wears out, no shift solenoids fail, and there is no torque converter to start slipping. Over very long distances the motor-generators and planetary gearset can see bearing wear and electrical ageing, but otherwise they hold up.

There is no scheduled replacement interval for an e-CVT. Belt-and-pulley CVTs have wear parts that an e-CVT doesn't. The e-CVT needs only the fluid service in the owner's manual and, for most Canadian owners, won't need replacing while they own the car. Buyers know this, and it is one of the main reasons Toyota and Lexus hybrids hold their value better than comparable gas-only models.

## Common questions

### Is a Toyota e-CVT the same as a regular CVT?

No. The names overlap, the hardware doesn't. A conventional [CVT](/glossary/cvt/) gets its continuously variable ratios from a steel push-belt between two variable-diameter pulleys. Toyota's e-CVT has neither. It is one planetary gearset connected to the gasoline engine and two electric motor-generators, and software varies the motor speeds. Behind the wheel it feels like a CVT, with no stepped climb in RPM, but the mechanism is a different design and a much more durable one.

### How long does a Toyota hybrid e-CVT last?

Very long, with normal maintenance. High-mileage Camry Hybrid and Prius taxis are the usual proof, and the e-CVT is rarely the part that fails first. Within a normal Canadian ownership period it has no scheduled replacement, unlike belt-and-pulley CVTs, which wear in ways an e-CVT doesn't. What helps most is following the fluid schedule in the owner's manual, especially the severe-service schedule if the car works hard.

### Does the e-CVT have a belt?

No, and this is the most common mix-up about Toyota's hybrid transmission. There is no belt, no pulley, no shift solenoid and no torque converter in an e-CVT. It is a single planetary gearset (the power-split device) plus two electric motor-generators. Software varies the motor-generator speeds to create the continuously variable behaviour; nothing mechanical slides along a pulley. That simple layout is why the e-CVT has lasted so well in Canadian taxi service.
