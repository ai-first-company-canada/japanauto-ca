## What is an EV?

An Electric Vehicle, technically a Battery Electric Vehicle (BEV), runs only on electricity stored in an onboard rechargeable battery. It has no internal-combustion engine, no fuel tank and no exhaust. There is no transmission in the usual sense either. A single-speed reduction gear usually connects the motor or motors to the drive wheels.

Next to Tesla, Hyundai or General Motors, the Japanese brands we list have a fairly short EV lineup in Canada. Nissan moved first with the Leaf, which arrived in Canada in 2011 and is now in its third generation (2026 model year). Other EVs sold new here include the Nissan Ariya, Toyota bZ4X, Lexus RZ, Subaru Solterra (a mechanical sibling of the bZ4X) and the Mazda MX-30, which Mazda Canada dropped after the 2024 model year. Honda's first Canadian EVs, the Honda Prologue and Acura ZDX, are built on General Motors' Ultium platform; the ZDX ended production in 2025.

Japanese EVs use different DC fast-charging connectors depending on age and model. First- and second-generation Nissan Leafs use CHAdeMO, the original Japanese standard, which is being phased out in North America. Most Japanese EVs from the early 2020s use CCS Combo. Newer models are switching to NACS, the Tesla-developed connector that most North American manufacturers have adopted; the third-generation Leaf already has a NACS port.

## Why it matters in Canada

In Canada, rebates do a lot to decide whether an EV makes financial sense, and they changed a lot in 2025 and 2026. The federal iZEV program ended in January 2025. The new federal Electric Vehicle Affordability Program covers new vehicles bought or leased on or after February 16, 2026: up to $5,000 on a BEV whose final transaction value is $50,000 or less, with no cap for vehicles made in Canada. The amount steps down each year (to $2,000 by 2030-2031), and the program runs to March 31, 2031 or until the money runs out. Quebec's Roulez vert adds up to $2,000 on a new BEV in 2026, its last year. British Columbia's Go Electric passenger rebate was paused in 2025. A qualifying new EV in Quebec can carry up to $7,000 in combined rebates in 2026.

Winter range loss is real, and it hits every battery chemistry. At -20°C, plan on 20 to 40 percent less range than the EPA or NRCan rating. The loss is worst at the lowest temperatures and in the first hour of driving, before the battery and cabin warm up. Preconditioning (running the battery's thermal management before you unplug) wins some of it back. For everyday ownership in Canada, a home Level-2 charger (240V, usually on a 30-amp circuit) is close to mandatory. You can get by on public DC fast chargers through a Canadian winter, but it costs more and cold weather slows them down.

## Common questions

### How much is the EV rebate in Canada?

The old federal iZEV rebate ended in January 2025. Its replacement, the Electric Vehicle Affordability Program (EVAP), applies to new vehicles bought or leased from February 16, 2026. In 2026 it pays up to $5,000 for Battery Electric and Fuel Cell vehicles and up to $2,500 for PHEVs, as long as the final transaction value is $50,000 or less (no cap on vehicles made in Canada). The amounts step down each year, and the program runs to March 31, 2031 or until funding runs out. Quebec's Roulez vert adds up to $2,000 on a new BEV in 2026 and ends December 31, 2026. British Columbia's Go Electric passenger rebate was paused in 2025. Used vehicles don't qualify for EVAP; Quebec offers a smaller used-EV rebate. Amounts change, so check tc.canada.ca and your province before you buy.

### How far does an EV go in Canadian winter?

Below -10°C, expect 20 to 40 percent less range than the EPA or NRCan rating, and the worst of it at -25°C or colder. A Nissan Leaf rated at 240 km combined will usually manage 145 to 180 km in a Calgary or Edmonton January. Preconditioning while still plugged in recovers some range. After the battery itself, cabin heat is the biggest drain in the cold, so using the heated seats and steering wheel instead of the cabin heater saves a noticeable amount.

### What is the difference between CHAdeMO and CCS?

CHAdeMO is the original Japanese DC fast-charging standard, introduced in 2010. In North America it is used mainly by the Nissan Leaf. CCS Combo (Combined Charging System) is the main North American standard, found on most newer Japanese, European and American EVs. Most North American manufacturers have adopted NACS, the Tesla-developed connector, and it is taking over from CCS Combo on new models. CHAdeMO plugs are getting harder to find at Canadian fast chargers.

### Which Japanese brands sell EVs in Canada?

On japanauto.ca, EVs from the Japanese brands we list include the Nissan Leaf and Ariya, Toyota bZ4X, Lexus RZ, Subaru Solterra (a mechanical sibling of the bZ4X), the Mazda MX-30 (sold here until the 2024 model year), and Honda's GM Ultium-based Prologue and Acura ZDX. Mitsubishi sells the Outlander PHEV in Canada but no pure EV. Acura, Infiniti and Lexus have small EV lineups, with Lexus's RZ the main one. Growth is fastest among small and mid-size crossover EVs priced from $40,000 to $60,000 MSRP. On a tight budget, a used Nissan Leaf in Vancouver or Toronto can be had for under $20,000.
