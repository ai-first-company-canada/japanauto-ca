## What is AWD?

All-Wheel Drive sends torque to all four wheels of a vehicle. That is the whole definition. When it does so, how much it sends and through what hardware vary a great deal from one system to the next, and that is where the marketing fog gathers.

On a Japanese crossover, modern AWD is almost always electronically controlled. Sensors read wheel speed, throttle, steering angle and yaw rate, and a clutch pack or differential decides how much torque goes to the rear. Some systems are reactive. They run as front-wheel drive until slip shows up, then engage the rear axle within milliseconds. Others drive all four wheels all the time by default; Subaru's [Symmetrical AWD](/glossary/symmetrical-awd/) is the obvious example. Both count as AWD, and they behave very differently on a slick on-ramp.

Full-time 4WD in pickups and traditional SUVs is a different thing. It uses a transfer case with low-range gearing for off-road work. A RAV4 has AWD. A 4Runner has 4WD with low range. A Subaru Outback sits somewhere between, and Subaru calls it AWD.

## Why it matters in Canada

Canadians buy a lot of AWD. Most new light vehicles sold here are SUVs, crossovers and pickups, and in snow-belt cities like Montreal, Calgary, Edmonton and Ottawa, used-car buyers often filter for AWD first. A used Japanese crossover with AWD usually asks more than the same model with FWD, partly because AWD was the costlier option when new. Compare like-for-like listings on japanauto.ca to see the gap for a given model and year.

AWD has one big limit, though: it does nothing for braking on snow or ice. Stopping comes down to the contact patch, and the contact patch comes down to your tires. In any controlled test, a FWD Civic on dedicated winter tires will out-stop an AWD Outback on all-seasons. Quebec law requires winter tires from December 1 to March 15 because the Highway Safety Code treats braking, not propulsion, as the thing that kills.

## Common questions

### Is AWD better than FWD for Canadian winters?

For getting moving from a stop on snow and ice, yes, especially on a hill or in deep snow where a FWD car would belly out. The advantage is real, and it's a big part of why Subaru sells well in snowy regions. Stopping is another matter, and AWD doesn't help there. If your city plows within hours of a snowfall, a FWD car on four winter tires will beat an AWD car on all-seasons on every measure that affects safety.

### Does AWD replace the need for winter tires?

No. Transport Canada's winter driving guidance and every major Canadian insurer say plainly that AWD is a propulsion system and winter tires are a traction system. They fix different problems. A winter tire's softer compound stays pliable below 7°C, and its tread is cut to bite into snow and slush. All-season tires harden in the cold and lose grip long before a winter tire does. Article 440.1 of the Quebec Highway Safety Code requires winter tires on every drivetrain.

### What is the difference between AWD and 4WD?

AWD systems usually have no transfer case and no low range. They split torque between the axles, often continuously or on demand, and are tuned for the road: snow, gravel, some light off-pavement use. 4WD locks the front and rear driveshafts together at a fixed ratio through a transfer case, and adds a low range for steep grades and rock crawling. A Toyota RAV4 is AWD; a Toyota Tacoma is 4WD. The difference shows up in towing capacity, articulation and crawl ratio off-road.

### Which Japanese brands offer AWD on every model?

Subaru is the only mainstream Japanese brand with [Symmetrical AWD](/glossary/symmetrical-awd/) standard on every model sold in Canada, with one exception: the rear-wheel-drive BRZ sports coupe. Mazda has AWD as standard or optional on the CX-5, CX-50, CX-70, CX-90 and some Mazda3 trims, though not the MX-5. Toyota, Honda, Nissan, Lexus, Acura, Infiniti and Mitsubishi put AWD on most crossovers and SUVs and keep entry sedans and hatchbacks FWD-only. Mitsubishi's [S-AWC](/glossary/s-awc/) is one of the most sophisticated AWD systems in the class.
