## What is a PHEV?

A Plug-in Hybrid Electric Vehicle is a hybrid with a larger traction battery that you can recharge from an external power source. The battery is usually 8 to 18 kWh. That's far bigger than the 1 to 2 kWh pack in a conventional hybrid and much smaller than the 60 to 100 kWh in a full EV. A PHEV gives real all-electric range, typically 30 to 80 km depending on model and conditions, and then switches to conventional hybrid operation once the battery runs down.

Living with one is easy. Plug in overnight at home, drive the next day on electricity, and the gas engine only starts if the trip runs past the electric range. Many Canadian commutes fit inside that range: downtown Toronto to Markham, downtown Vancouver to Burnaby, the Plateau to the West Island in Montreal. On routes like those, the engine may not run for weeks.

Among the Japanese brands we list, Canadian PHEV choices are the Toyota Prius Prime, Toyota RAV4 Prime, Lexus NX 450h+, Lexus RX 450h+ and Mitsubishi Outlander PHEV. The Outlander PHEV, RAV4 Prime and NX 450h+ all have all-wheel drive; the Outlander's is Mitsubishi's twin-motor [S-AWC](/glossary/s-awc/).

## Why it matters in Canada

The rebate picture changed in 2025 and 2026. The federal iZEV rebate ended in January 2025. Its replacement, the Electric Vehicle Affordability Program, pays up to $2,500 on a new PHEV bought or leased from February 16, 2026, if the final transaction value is $50,000 or less (no cap on vehicles made in Canada). That amount steps down each year to $1,000 by 2030-2031, and the program runs to March 31, 2031. Quebec's Roulez vert adds up to $1,000 on a new PHEV with a battery of 15 kWh or more ($500 for 8 to 15 kWh) until the program ends on December 31, 2026. British Columbia's Go Electric passenger rebate was paused in 2025.

The big draw in Canada is that a PHEV takes charging out of the equation. Someone in a downtown Toronto condo with no dedicated parking can run a PHEV on gasoline indefinitely if plugging in isn't reliable, where an EV wouldn't work for them. A Calgary homeowner with a Level-2 charger in the garage drives on electricity every day and still takes the Trans-Canada on road trips without planning around chargers. How much you save depends on your access to charging. Either way, a PHEV covers more situations than a plain hybrid or a pure EV.

## Common questions

### What is the difference between a hybrid and a PHEV?

A conventional [hybrid](/glossary/hybrid/) charges its small battery (1 to 2 kWh) from regenerative braking and the gas engine. There is no plug. A PHEV has a much bigger battery (8 to 18 kWh) that you charge from an outside source, good for 30 to 80 km on electricity before the engine starts. New PHEVs can qualify for the federal EV incentive and conventional hybrids can't. To get the electric-driving benefit, a PHEV needs access to charging (home Level-2 is ideal, public Level-2 works). A conventional hybrid needs no charging at all.

### Does a PHEV qualify for the Canadian iZEV rebate?

Not any more. iZEV ended in January 2025. The program that replaced it, the Electric Vehicle Affordability Program, pays up to $2,500 on a new PHEV bought or leased on or after February 16, 2026, as long as the final transaction value is $50,000 or less (vehicles made in Canada have no cap). The amount steps down each year and the program runs to March 31, 2031. Quebec's Roulez vert adds up to $1,000 in 2026 for a PHEV with a battery of 15 kWh or more. British Columbia's Go Electric passenger rebate is paused. Used PHEVs don't qualify federally. Check current eligibility at tc.canada.ca before you buy.

### How far can a PHEV drive on electric only?

It depends on the model and the weather. Current Mitsubishi Outlander PHEV, Toyota RAV4 Prime and Toyota Prius Prime models are rated at roughly 60 to 70 km; older Outlander PHEVs and Prius Primes are rated much lower. In the cold, expect the same kind of loss EVs see, often 20 to 40 percent below the rating at -20°C. Preconditioning the battery while plugged in wins back some range. Heating the cabin uses more battery energy than moving the car.

### Do I need a home charger for a PHEV?

For most real Canadian use, effectively yes. A standard 120V outlet (Level-1) takes 8 to 12 hours to refill a PHEV battery from empty. That's fine overnight if your commute is shorter than your electric range, and slow if you burn through the range more than once a day. A 240V Level-2 home charger (usually a 30-amp circuit, like an electric dryer's) does it in 2 to 4 hours and is the practical choice for Canadian owners. Toronto, Vancouver and Montreal have enough public Level-2 chargers to serve as backup. And if you're fine running on gasoline when the battery is empty, you can own a PHEV with no home charging at all.
