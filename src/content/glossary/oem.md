## What is OEM?

An OEM (Original Equipment Manufacturer) part is a replacement component built to the exact specification of the part the vehicle had when it left the factory. Same engineering tolerances, same materials, and in many cases the same supplier. Order an OEM brake pad for a 2018 Toyota Camry and you get the part that went onto new Camrys at the factory that year (Canadian-market Camrys come from Toyota's Kentucky plant).

OEM and aftermarket overlap more than people think. Toyota, Honda and Nissan buy many of their factory components from outside suppliers such as Denso, Aisin, NGK, KYB and Bridgestone, and those suppliers sell the same parts into the aftermarket under their own names. A Denso oxygen sensor sold by a Toyota dealer is OEM. The identical Denso sensor sold on PartsAvatar.ca in a Denso box is technically aftermarket, and it works exactly the same. It can cost 30 to 60 percent less. For Japanese cars in Canada, that's one of the real bargains in the parts market.

Where aftermarket quality does fall off is the no-name stuff. Brake pads, oxygen sensors and timing parts from unknown makers vary widely, and on safety-critical systems the savings over a known OEM supplier brand are rarely worth it.

## Why it matters in Canada

Canadian dealers for the nine Japanese brands sold here (Toyota, Honda, Nissan, Mazda, Subaru, Lexus, Acura, Infiniti, Mitsubishi) honour OEM-part warranties when a licensed shop installs the part, typically for 12 months or 20,000 kilometres. The warranty stays with the part. If a dealer-installed OEM water pump fails at 18 months on a car that has since been sold, the second owner can still claim. Aftermarket parts rarely give you that.

[JDM](/glossary/jdm/) imports have one Canadian quirk. A car brought in from Japan under the 15-year rule may carry Japan-market part numbers that don't map cleanly to the Canadian-spec ones. The hardware is usually identical, but a Toyota Canada dealer's parts catalogue is keyed to the [VIN](/glossary/vin/) of a Canadian-market car. Owners of JDM imports should expect to buy some parts through a JDM specialist (Amayama, Megazip), or take the part number to the dealer parts counter and have them cross-reference it by hand.

## Common questions

### Are OEM parts always better than aftermarket parts?

On safety-critical parts (brakes, suspension, airbag modules, anything in the steering or fuel system), OEM is the safer default. Quality control is tighter, and the tolerances are validated against the rest of the car. For consumables like cabin air filters, wiper blades or interior trim, an OE-equivalent aftermarket part from a brand that supplies the factory (Denso, NGK, Aisin, KYB) is usually better value at 40 to 60 percent of the dealer price. A good rule: if a failure could hurt someone, buy OEM. If it would only be a nuisance, a known-supplier aftermarket part is fine.

### Do OEM parts come with a warranty in Canada?

Yes. Most Canadian dealers of Japanese brands warranty an OEM part installed by a licensed shop for 12 months or 20,000 kilometres, whichever comes first. That covers the part and the labour to replace it, provided the part itself failed and not the installation or something external. Aftermarket warranties exist but differ by brand and retailer. RockAuto, PartsAvatar and PartsEngine Canada each have their own policies, and they usually cover the part only, without labour.

### Where can I buy OEM parts for a Japanese car in Canada?

Straight from the manufacturer's Canadian parts portal (parts.toyota.ca, parts.honda.ca, parts.nissan.ca and the equivalents for the other six brands), or at the parts counter of any same-brand dealer in your city. Most dealers will also order in parts they don't stock, usually in one to three days in the six largest metro areas. For JDM-spec cars, Amayama and Megazip ship genuine OEM parts from Japan to Canadian addresses, typically in 7 to 14 days. CBSA clears them like any other imported parcel, and duty treatment for goods from Japan falls under the CPTPP trade agreement, not CUSMA.

### Do OEM parts from Japan fit a Canadian-spec car?

Often yes, sometimes no. Mechanical parts (engine internals, transmission internals, suspension hardware on a model sold in both markets) are usually the same, and the Japanese OEM part will fit. Body panels, lighting assemblies, electronics and HVAC components are more likely to differ, because Canadian-market cars must meet Canadian Motor Vehicle Safety Standards that don't match Japan's. Before ordering an OEM-Japan part for a Canadian-market car, have a dealer parts counter cross-reference it against your [VIN](/glossary/vin/).
