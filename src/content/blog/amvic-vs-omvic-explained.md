If you are buying a used Japanese vehicle in Calgary or Toronto, you are dealing with a kind of regulator most provinces don't have. Alberta and Ontario, along with British Columbia, hand car-retail regulation to a not-for-profit body funded by the industry: [AMVIC](/glossary/amvic/) in Alberta, [OMVIC](/glossary/omvic/) in Ontario. The two work in much the same way. Their laws, fees, compensation fund limits and disclosure rules are not the same, though, and the differences can matter to you.

Below: what each regulator does, what it protects you from, where the rules split, and what to watch if you shop across the provincial line.

## What is AMVIC and what does it regulate?

AMVIC stands for the Alberta Motor Vehicle Industry Council. It was established in 1999 as a delegated administrative authority under Alberta's Consumer Protection Act and Automotive Business Regulation, with responsibility for licensing the businesses that sell, lease, consign, buy and repair vehicles in Alberta, along with the salespeople who work for them. Nationally, that set-up is unusual but not unique: Ontario (OMVIC) and British Columbia (the Vehicle Sales Authority) also hand dealer regulation to arm's-length, industry-funded bodies, while most other provinces handle it inside government.

A Calgary or Edmonton dealership with a service department typically holds both a sales licence and a repair licence, and its salespeople hold their own licences. Alberta dealer ads, including online listings on japanauto.ca, have to make clear they come from an AMVIC-licensed business. To check a licence, use the "Find a Licensed Business" tool at amvic.org. It shows licence type, current status, expiry date, registered business address, and any disciplinary history.

AMVIC's enforcement authority includes investigating consumer complaints, prosecuting unlicensed sellers (curbsiders) under the Consumer Protection Act, and administering a consumer compensation fund. The compensation fund is industry-financed and reimburses Alberta consumers for verifiable financial losses caused by AMVIC-licensed dealers, up to a per-claim maximum set by the AMVIC board.

## What is OMVIC and what does it regulate?

OMVIC stands for the Ontario Motor Vehicle Industry Council. It was established in 1997 under what became the Motor Vehicle Dealers Act, 2002, and operates as Ontario's delegated administrative authority for motor-vehicle dealer regulation. It looks a lot like AMVIC: a not-for-profit funded by the industry, run by a board that includes public members, and licensing every dealer and salesperson in the province.

OMVIC's responsibilities include licensing every motor-vehicle dealer and salesperson in Ontario, administering a mandatory salesperson certification course (the Automotive Certification Course), enforcing the Motor Vehicle Dealers Act, and operating the Motor Vehicle Dealers Compensation Fund. The fund pays up to $45,000 CAD per claim.

Like AMVIC, OMVIC has advertising rules, and an Ontario dealer has to advertise under the name it's registered with. The dealer search at omvic.ca returns the legal business name, registration status, expiry date, registered business address, and any public disciplinary history. One difference from AMVIC: OMVIC doesn't license repair shops or vehicle inspectors. Ontario regulates those under other rules.

## How do AMVIC and OMVIC compensation funds compare?

For buyers, this is the part that matters most. The OMVIC Motor Vehicle Dealers Compensation Fund pays up to $45,000 CAD per claim. The AMVIC consumer compensation fund pays up to a per-claim maximum set by the AMVIC board, which has historically been a different figure than OMVIC's.

Eligibility criteria are similar but not identical. Both cover losses from a licensed dealer's misconduct: an undisclosed lien, a car that never arrived after you paid a deposit, or misrepresentation the regulator upheld after investigating. Neither covers private sales, curbsiders or other unlicensed sellers, or cases where you skipped the regulator's dispute process. Both want proof of the sale and the loss, and proof you tried to settle with the dealer first.

In practice, the cap rarely decides a claim. What usually decides it is whether your loss qualifies at all. Read the fund eligibility criteria at amvic.org or omvic.ca before assuming the fund will cover a specific dispute.

## Disclosure obligations: where the rules differ

Both provinces make dealers disclose the basics on a used vehicle: accident history they know about, registered liens, salvage or rebuilt brands, odometer history and warranty status. How they disclose it differs.

OMVIC dealers are required by the Motor Vehicle Dealers Act to provide a written contract that includes specific disclosure items, plus a Bill of Sale that serves as the primary transaction document. Ontario also makes private sellers hand over a [UVIP](/glossary/uvip/) (Used Vehicle Information Package), a separate document ServiceOntario generates from the central registration database. Dealers are not required to provide a UVIP, though some Ontario dealers will obtain one as part of their disclosure documentation.

AMVIC dealers are required by the Consumer Protection Act and Automotive Business Regulation to provide written disclosure of material information including accident history known to the dealer, lien status, and any salvage or rebuilt branding. Alberta is less fussy about format than Ontario, but what dealers must disclose is about the same. There's no Alberta UVIP for private sales, though, so if you buy privately in Alberta, run your own [Lien](/glossary/lien/) search through the Alberta Personal Property Registry.

## Cross-province transactions: who regulates what?

Buyers who shop across provinces mix this up all the time. Short answer: each province's regulator covers transactions completed under that province's licensing, no matter where the buyer registers the vehicle afterwards.

If you buy from an Ontario dealer for delivery to Alberta, OMVIC's regulatory framework and compensation fund apply to the dealer's conduct under Ontario law. If you buy from an Alberta dealer for delivery to Ontario, AMVIC's framework applies to the dealer's conduct under Alberta law. Crossing a border can also complicate a fund claim, so read the eligibility rules of the dealer's province before you buy.

Our advice: buy from a dealer in your own province when you can. An out-of-province deal can work, but you lose some of the safety net, and the [Provincial Safety Inspection](/glossary/provincial-safety-inspection/) requirements add cost (Alberta's [Out-of-Province Inspection](/glossary/provincial-safety-inspection/) is required before most vehicles from outside Alberta can be registered there).

## What about private sellers and curbsiders?

Both AMVIC and OMVIC apply only to licensed dealers and the businesses they regulate. Someone selling their own car, not running a business, is outside both regulators' reach.

That distinction matters because Alberta and Ontario both have curbsiding problems: unlicensed sellers operating as de facto dealers without obtaining the AMVIC or OMVIC licence the activity requires. Curbsiders typically buy vehicles at auction, present them as personal sales on Kijiji or Facebook Marketplace, and avoid the disclosure obligations that licensed dealers must follow. Both regulators go after curbsiders, with tickets, charges and fines under Alberta's Consumer Protection Act and Ontario's Motor Vehicle Dealers Act.

The giveaway is volume. A "private seller" advertising multiple vehicles, or one whose vehicle has been listed multiple times in recent months under different sellers, is likely a curbsider. Walk away. The regulator protections that apply to licensed dealer transactions do not exist with curbsiders.

## How does British Columbia fit into this picture?

The third Canadian provincial dealer regulator is the [Vehicle Sales Authority of British Columbia](/glossary/vsa/), abbreviated VSA. Set up in 2004 under the Motor Dealer Act, it follows the same model as AMVIC and OMVIC. The industry pays for it, it licenses dealers and salespeople, and it runs a compensation fund. It covers Vancouver, Victoria and the rest of BC.

Quebec, Saskatchewan, Manitoba and the Atlantic provinces regulate car sales through government rather than delegated authorities. In Quebec, used-car dealers need a permit from the Office de la protection du consommateur (OPC). Disclosure rules and consumer protection vary by province but generally exist in some form under provincial consumer protection legislation.

If you're shopping in more than one province, say Toronto and Calgary, or Vancouver and Edmonton, find out which regulator covers the seller before you commit. Verification tools, disclosure rules and your options for recourse all change at the border.

## Common questions

### Is AMVIC the same as OMVIC?

No, they are separate provincial regulators. [AMVIC](/glossary/amvic/) regulates Alberta under the Consumer Protection Act and Automotive Business Regulation. [OMVIC](/glossary/omvic/) regulates Ontario under the Motor Vehicle Dealers Act, 2002. Each issues its own licences, runs its own compensation fund and sets its own disclosure rules. Both are not-for-profit and industry-funded, but licences, fees and fund details differ. A dealer licensed by one regulator has no automatic standing in the other province.

### Which fund pays more, AMVIC or OMVIC?

The OMVIC Motor Vehicle Dealers Compensation Fund pays up to $45,000 CAD per claim. The AMVIC consumer compensation fund pays up to a per-claim maximum set by the AMVIC board. In most disputes the cap isn't the problem. Eligibility is. Verify current fund rules at amvic.org or omvic.ca before assuming a specific dispute is compensable.

### Can an Ontario dealer sell in Alberta?

Not without obtaining a separate AMVIC licence to operate in Alberta. Each provincial regulator licenses only dealers operating in its own province. An Ontario dealer that wants to sell vehicles to Alberta buyers can ship vehicles for out-of-province delivery, but Ontario rules govern the sale. OMVIC oversees it, and Alberta protections such as the AMVIC compensation fund don't apply. You can buy that way, with less protection. Buying from a dealer in your own province is simpler.

### Do private sellers fall under AMVIC or OMVIC?

No. Both regulators apply only to licensed dealers and the businesses they regulate. A person selling their own car, not as a business, falls outside both, and a private buyer can't claim against either compensation fund. That gap is part of why Ontario requires the [UVIP](/glossary/uvip/): it gives private buyers the registry information a licensed dealer would otherwise have to disclose. Alberta has no UVIP equivalent for private sales.

### Which regulator covers JDM imports?

[JDM](/glossary/jdm/) imports brought in under the federal 15-year rule are covered by whichever provincial regulator licenses the dealer involved in the sale. A JDM import sold by an OMVIC-registered Ontario dealer falls under OMVIC's framework. The same vehicle sold by an AMVIC-licensed Alberta dealer falls under AMVIC's framework. Federal rules and customs cover the import itself (a 15-year-old car from Japan doesn't go through the [RIV Program](/glossary/riv-program/), which handles newer US vehicles). The provincial regulator deals with the retail sale. Private-sale JDM imports are outside both provincial regulatory frameworks and require buyer-side due diligence including pre-purchase inspection and Japanese auction-grade verification.

This information reflects regulations effective May 2026 and provincial sources cited above. For a specific transaction, contact AMVIC or OMVIC directly, talk to a lawyer, or call your provincial consumer protection office. The regulators license dealers and salespeople; they don't license advisors.
