Three crossovers dominate the Canadian compact-SUV used market: the Toyota RAV4, the Honda CR-V and the Mazda CX-5. All three are top-10 sellers with clean reliability records, and you can get AWD on most trims of each. Which one suits you comes down to differences the ads tend to blur.

We compare the 2018-2022 generations as used buys in 2026 on price in the six Tier-1 cities, winter AWD, hybrid options, cabin and cargo, and long-term cost, and say which one we'd buy for which kind of owner.

## What they cost in 2026

A 2019 Toyota RAV4 LE AWD with 90,000 to 110,000 km in Toronto, Vancouver, or Calgary lists between $26,000 and $30,000. The same model and year of Honda CR-V LX AWD lists between $25,000 and $29,000. That's usually a bit under the RAV4, though in Vancouver, where CR-V demand is strong, it's sometimes higher. A Mazda CX-5 GS AWD of the same year typically lists between $22,500 and $26,500, about $2,500 below the CR-V and $3,500 below the RAV4 at equivalent trim and kilometres.

Depreciation explains the gap, and the pattern has held for years. RAV4 retains around 70 percent of MSRP at three years; CR-V around 65 to 68 percent; CX-5 around 58 to 62 percent. So the RAV4 costs the most used even though the three do much the same job. Selling in 3 to 5 years? The RAV4 gets you more back. Keeping it 8 to 10 years? The CX-5's discount keeps paying off the longer you own it.

Older, higher-kilometre cars (2018-2020, 130,000+ km) bunch together: $19,000 to $23,000 across the Tier-1 markets for clean examples in mid-trim. At that point the CX-5's price edge mostly disappears, and trim and condition matter more than the badge.

## AWD and winter driving

All three sell AWD on their main Canadian trims, and the systems differ. Toyota's Dynamic Torque Control AWD on the RAV4 (and the fancier Dynamic Torque Vectoring AWD on some trims) is reactive. It drives the front wheels and brings in the rear axle through an electronic coupling when sensors detect slip or unusual inputs. Honda's Real Time AWD on the CR-V works much the same way, through an electronic clutch. Mazda's [i-Activ AWD](/glossary/awd/) on the CX-5 is also reactive but with unusually aggressive preemptive calibration, engaging the rear axle under cornering loads and throttle transitions before slip occurs.

On plowed roads in Toronto, Vancouver, Calgary or any of the other Tier-1 cities, you won't notice much difference. On good winter tires, all three pull away cleanly on packed snow, corner and stop on ice if you're careful, and have well-tuned stability control. In the city, the tires you put on matter far more than which of the three AWD systems you pick.

For deep-snow accumulation, rural driving, or variable-grip conditions, none of the three matches Subaru's [Symmetrical AWD](/glossary/symmetrical-awd/) for absolute capability. Between these three, the Mazda reacts earliest, the RAV4 has the best low-speed traction control, and the CR-V sits in the middle. Instrumented testing can measure the gaps. On a real Canadian road, on real winter tires, they're small.

## Hybrids: where they really differ

Toyota has the most hybrid choice. Used RAV4 Hybrids (gas-electric, no plug) are easy to find across Canada. The RAV4 Prime ([PHEV](/glossary/phev/), about 68 km rated electric range on 2021-2024 models) qualified for the federal iZEV rebate of up to $5,000 when bought new, until iZEV closed in January 2025. Used buyers never got the rebate, though it shaped what the cars sell for now. Both cost noticeably more than the gas RAV4: $2,000 to $3,500 for the RAV4 Hybrid, $5,000 to $9,000 for the RAV4 Prime PHEV.

Honda's [i-MMD](/glossary/i-mmd/) hybrid system on the CR-V Hybrid arrived in Canada for the 2020 model year, and a modest number are now for sale used. It pairs a 2.0L Atkinson-cycle engine with two electric motors and works differently from Toyota Hybrid Synergy Drive. Used pricing on the CR-V Hybrid typically runs $1,500 to $3,000 above the comparable gas trim. Honda has not offered a CR-V PHEV in Canada as of 2026.

Mazda doesn't sell a hybrid CX-5 in Canada. The Alabama-built CX-50 added a Toyota-sourced hybrid for 2025, and the three-row CX-90 offers a plug-in hybrid, but both are newer and cost more than these 2018-2022 cars. Among the three models compared here, a used hybrid means a RAV4 or a CR-V.

## Cabin and cargo

In mid-trim, the CX-5 has the nicest cabin. Mazda spent heavily on materials from the 2017 redesign on, and a 2019 CX-5 GT or Signature has leather, real metal trim and a steering wheel that a same-year CR-V or RAV4 at the same trim level can't match. You pay for it in cargo space, which is noticeably smaller than the CR-V's. A single buyer or a couple will appreciate the cabin. A young family with a stroller and a weekly Costco run will want the CR-V.

The CR-V offers the largest interior volume in the segment and the most refined ride for highway distance. With the 2018-2022 generation Honda tuned for comfort and quiet over driving feel, which suits Canadian commuters. Cargo capacity with the rear seats folded is class-leading among the three, and the rear seat slides forward and reclines on most trims.

The RAV4 sits between the two. Cargo volume is closer to the CR-V's than the CX-5's. Cabin materials in mid-trim are below the CX-5 GT but above the entry CR-V LX. The 2019 redesign gave it a tougher look, especially in Adventure and TRD Off-Road trims, which caught on with Canadian buyers under 40. Those trims sell for a bit more used.

## What it costs over 10 years

Keep it 8 to 10 years and the CX-5 usually wins. You pay less up front and about the same for maintenance (long-term reliability is similar across the three), so over a decade it often costs $3,000 to $6,000 less than the equivalent RAV4, assuming you sell at the end. If you'll drive it until it dies, depreciation doesn't matter at all.

Over 3 to 5 years, the RAV4's stronger resale usually makes up for its higher price, so it's close to a wash. The CR-V lands in between, with decent resale and middling prices. It's neither the bargain nor the resale champion.

Beyond depreciation, running costs are close. Service intervals, dealer parts prices and OEM-equivalent parts (Denso, NGK, Aisin, KYB) differ only a little ([OEM glossary](/glossary/oem/)). Mazda has fewer dealers than Toyota or Honda, which can matter in a small town but not in any of the six Tier-1 cities.

## Common questions

### Which compact SUV holds value best in Canada?

The Toyota RAV4. It keeps roughly 70 percent of original MSRP after three years in Canadian Black Book averages, the best of the three. The Honda CR-V follows at 65 to 68 percent. The Mazda CX-5 trails at 58 to 62 percent. That order reflects how much buyers want each badge, and it has held across several generations. Sell in 3 to 5 years and the RAV4 returns the most. Keep it 8 to 10 years and the cheaper CX-5 often costs least overall.

### Is CX-5 reliable compared to CR-V and RAV4?

Yes, about the same. The 2.5L [SkyActiv](/glossary/skyactiv/)-G in modern CX-5s regularly passes 300,000 km in Canada, and no major failure mode has turned up in Transport Canada's defects database for cars built since the 2017 refresh. The Honda 1.5L L15 turbo in the CR-V is similarly durable once past its early oil-dilution problem (2017-2018 CR-Vs, fixed by Honda Canada with a software update and extended warranty). The Toyota 2.5L A25A in the 2019-and-newer RAV4 is among the most-tested Japanese powertrains in Canadian production. All three are reliable. What separates their long-term cost is depreciation and trim pricing, not breakdowns.

### Which is best for Canadian winters?

In a Canadian city, on proper winter tires, it's a tie. Mazda's [i-Activ AWD](/glossary/awd/) has a slight edge in preemptive engagement on cornering and throttle transitions. Toyota's Dynamic Torque Vectoring AWD on certain RAV4 trims has a slight edge in low-speed traction calibration. Honda's Real Time AWD is the most middle-of-the-road. None matches Subaru [Symmetrical AWD](/glossary/symmetrical-awd/) for deep-snow capability. For city driving, choose on cabin, cargo space and price, not on AWD.

### Hybrid vs gas: which makes sense in 2026?

For a heavy commuter in Toronto, Vancouver or Montreal, a hybrid pays back the $1,500 to $3,500 premium within 80,000 to 120,000 km of typical commuter use. Toyota Hybrid Synergy Drive on the RAV4 Hybrid is the broadest hybrid offering. Honda's [i-MMD](/glossary/i-mmd/) on the CR-V Hybrid works differently and is just as good. The Mazda CX-5 does not offer a hybrid trim in the Canadian lineup. If you drive little, live rurally, or sell after 3 to 4 years, buy gas. If you want a plug-in with AWD, the RAV4 Prime is the best Japanese option in 2026.

### What's the price difference between the three?

For 2019 mid-trim AWD at 90,000 to 110,000 km in 2026: RAV4 LE AWD typically lists $26,000 to $30,000, CR-V LX AWD lists $25,000 to $29,000, CX-5 GS AWD lists $22,500 to $26,500. The CX-5 runs about $2,500 below a comparable CR-V and $3,500 below a comparable RAV4 in most markets. At higher kilometres (130,000+), the band compresses to roughly $19,000 to $23,000 across all three platforms. Hybrid trims add $1,500 to $3,500 (RAV4 Hybrid, CR-V Hybrid). The RAV4 Prime PHEV adds $5,000 to $9,000 over the equivalent gas trim.
