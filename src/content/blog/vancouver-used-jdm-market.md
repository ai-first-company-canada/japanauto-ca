For JDM cars, Vancouver is Canada's front door. Most cars shipped from Japan land at the Port of Vancouver, and brokers in Richmond and Burnaby have been clearing 15-year imports for two decades. BC enthusiasts and small businesses also want kei trucks, performance imports and oddball Japanese vehicles in bigger numbers than anywhere else in Canada. Whatever you want a used [JDM](/glossary/jdm/) car for in 2026, from commuting to a weekend project to a work truck, Vancouver has the most to choose from.

Below: how importing works, which auction grades to buy, what it costs landed, and the BC registration and insurance rules that matter if you plan to drive it daily.

## How does the JDM import process work in 2026?

Under federal rules a vehicle must be fifteen years past its month of manufacture. In 2026 that means a JDM car built by the same month of 2011 can be imported. At that age it's exempt from the Canadian Motor Vehicle Safety Standards and doesn't go through the federal [RIV Program](/glossary/riv-program/), which handles US vehicles under 15. It still has to pass BC's inspection before it can be registered. Expect 6 to 12 weeks from a Japanese auction win to BC plates.

It starts with the purchase. Most JDM imports are bought at Japanese auto auctions, mainly through the USS, JU and TAA networks. A Vancouver-based import broker bids on the buyer's behalf, wins the auction, arranges shipping by roll-on roll-off (RoRo) ship from Yokohama or Nagoya, and handles documentation through customs clearance. Brokers typically charge $1,500 to $3,500 CAD service fees on top of the auction price, shipping costs, and CBSA duties.

Next comes customs. When the car arrives in Vancouver, the broker completes the Vehicle Import Form (Form 1) with the Canada Border Services Agency and pays what's owed at the border. A Japan-built car can enter duty-free under the CPTPP trade agreement with a certification of origin; otherwise 6.1% duty applies. CUSMA doesn't cover Japanese-built cars. There's also a $100 excise tax on cars with air conditioning, and 5% GST. No RIV fee, no federal modifications and no Canadian Tire inspection apply to a 15-year-old car. If you want to check for open recalls, a same-brand dealer can look up the [VIN](/glossary/vin/) or chassis number, though many JDM-only models won't be in Canadian systems.

Last is BC registration. Vehicles from outside BC need a Designated Inspection Facility inspection, and repairs if it fails. Then you register and plate the car through an ICBC Autoplan broker, pay PST at that point, and arrange insurance.

## What is the total cost of importing a JDM vehicle?

The auction price is only the start. A typical JDM Honda Acty kei truck purchased at auction in Japan for ¥350,000 (roughly CAD $3,200) lands in Vancouver at approximately CAD $7,500 to $9,500 all-in including shipping, broker fees, customs, GST, BC PST, inspection and registration. On a truck that cheap, shipping and broker fees cost about as much as the truck, so the all-in price can be more than double the hammer price.

On pricier imports such as a Nissan Skyline GT-R R32/R33/R34, Toyota Chaser JZX100 or Honda Beat, the fixed costs matter less and taxes matter more. (The Honda S660, built from 2015, won't be importable until about 2030.) A clean R32 GT-R purchased at auction for ¥5 million (roughly CAD $46,000) typically lands in Vancouver at CAD $58,000 to $66,000 all-in. The same R32 GT-R on a Vancouver dealer's lot typically runs CAD $70,000 to $90,000. The difference is the dealer's markup for handling the import.

Here's everything the buyer pays for:
- Auction hammer price (the headline number)
- Japanese auction broker commission (typically ¥30,000 to ¥80,000)
- Domestic transport in Japan to the export port
- RoRo shipping (typically CAD $1,800 to $3,500 for a passenger vehicle)
- Marine insurance during transit
- CBSA duty (zero with CPTPP origin documents, otherwise 6.1%)
- $100 federal excise tax if the car has air conditioning
- GST (5% federal), paid at the border
- BC PST, paid when you register (7% for a passenger vehicle under $55,000 imported from outside Canada, rising with price)
- BC Designated Inspection Facility inspection ($250 to $500) and any repairs it calls for
- BC registration and ICBC insurance

On your first import, use a broker who handles the whole chain. You can do it yourself and typically save $2,000 to $4,000, but there's more to get wrong.

## What auction grade should I look for?

Most North American buyers underrate how much the Japanese auction grade tells them. Numeric grades run from "S" or 6 (effectively new) down through 5, 4.5, 4, 3.5, 3, 2 and 1, and letter grades such as R, RA and A flag accident damage or repair history.

If you want a clean daily driver, buy grade 4 or better. A grade 4 vehicle has minor cosmetic flaws but no mechanical issues or significant damage history. Grade 4.5 and 5 are progressively cleaner. Grade 3 and below usually means documented repairs, doubtful mileage or mechanical problems. Those cars can be cheap project material, but they're a poor first import.

The letter grades (R, RA, A) mean the car has accident damage or repair history; the exact meaning varies a little between auction houses, and the auction sheet says what was damaged and where. None of them leads to a Canadian [Salvage Title](/glossary/salvage-title/) brand, because the damage happened in Japan, not through a Canadian insurance write-off. That's why every JDM import needs a pre-purchase inspection and a paint-thickness gauge check on the panels, however clean the CarFax looks.

Auction sheets contain detailed condition diagrams marking specific panel damage, paint repair, frame condition, and interior wear. A reputable Vancouver broker will give you the sheet and an English translation before you buy. If a broker can't or won't, find another one.

## Are right-hand-drive cars legal in BC?

Yes, with some catches. Right-hand-drive vehicles imported under the 15-year rule are legal for road registration in British Columbia once they pass the provincial inspection. The Vehicle Sales Authority of British Columbia ([VSA](/glossary/vsa/)) licenses dealers and salespeople; it doesn't regulate which vehicles can be registered, so it adds no RHD rules of its own. ICBC will register RHD vehicles without RHD-specific surcharges in most cases.

The catches are insurance and daily use. ICBC's basic Autoplan coverage is available on RHD vehicles, but optional collision coverage may be quoted at higher rates than equivalent left-hand-drive vehicles. Some optional-coverage providers operating in BC decline RHD coverage entirely. Confirm coverage with your insurer before importing.

Day to day, sitting on the right is a nuisance. Tollbooths, parkade ticket machines and drive-throughs are all on the wrong side. Passing on a two-lane road is harder because you're sitting on the wrong side to see past the car ahead. That's why many Vancouver owners keep RHD cars for weekends and projects instead of the daily commute.

## Daily-driver vs project car: how to think about JDM ownership

First-time buyers tend to underestimate how different daily-driver and weekend-project ownership are. A daily driver makes sense when the car was also sold elsewhere in the world, like a Japanese-spec Toyota Corolla, Honda Civic or Nissan Pathfinder. JDM-only models such as the Toyota Chaser, Honda Acty or Nissan Skyline GT-R are project cars: parts, mechanics and Canadian insurance all reflect how rare they are here.

A JDM daily driver in Vancouver works if you're fine sitting on the right, have a second car for bad weather, know an independent shop that services JDM cars (Vancouver has several), and don't mind ordering some parts from Japanese specialty sellers like Amayama or Megazip instead of a Canadian dealer. If that's not you, buy a Canadian-spec used Japanese car for daily driving.

A project car is a different arrangement. It comes out on weekends, hunting for parts is half the fun, and the Canadian market for clean specialty JDM cars is established enough that the right car, kept a few years, can hold or even gain value. Vancouver's JDM enthusiast community is the largest in Canada and supports both casual and serious project-car ownership.

## Common questions

### How long does it take to import a JDM car to Vancouver?

Typical timeline from auction purchase to BC plates is 6 to 12 weeks. Roughly: 1 to 3 weeks for Japanese auction processing and domestic transport to export port, 3 to 5 weeks for RoRo shipping across the Pacific, then port handling and CBSA clearance, and 1 to 2 weeks for the BC Designated Inspection Facility inspection, any repairs, and ICBC registration. A 15-year-old car skips the federal [RIV Program](/glossary/riv-program/) and its Canadian Tire inspection. In spring and summer ships and inspection slots fill up and everything takes longer. If you're on a deadline, plan for 12 to 16 weeks, not 6.

### What is the total cost of importing a JDM vehicle?

It depends on the car, because fixed costs weigh more on cheap ones. For a kei truck bought at auction for about CAD $3,200, expect $7,500 to $9,500 all-in. For a clean R32 GT-R at about CAD $46,000 at auction, expect $58,000 to $66,000 all-in. Cost categories include broker commission, RoRo shipping, marine insurance, CBSA duty if CPTPP origin isn't documented, the $100 air-conditioning excise tax, GST, BC PST at registration, the BC Designated Inspection Facility inspection and repairs, and ICBC registration. There's no RIV fee on a 15-year-old import. Budget for the top of the range on your first import. Experienced importers with a broker they trust sometimes hit the bottom.

### Are right-hand-drive cars legal in BC?

Yes. Right-hand-drive vehicles imported under the federal 15-year rule are legal for BC road registration once they pass the provincial inspection. The [VSA](/glossary/vsa/) licenses dealers, not vehicles, so it has no RHD rules of its own. ICBC writes basic Autoplan coverage on RHD vehicles. Optional collision and comprehensive coverage may be available at standard or modestly elevated rates depending on insurer. Some optional-coverage providers decline RHD coverage entirely. Verify insurance availability before importing.

### What auction grade should I look for on a JDM import?

For a clean daily-driver JDM, target grade 4 or higher on Japanese auction grading. Grade 4 indicates minor cosmetic flaws but no mechanical issues or significant damage. Grades 4.5, 5, 6, and S are progressively cleaner. Grade 3 and below usually means documented repairs, doubtful mileage or mechanical problems: fine for a project, wrong for a first import. Letter grades such as R, RA and A flag accident damage or repair history; read the auction sheet for the details. None of these auction grades trigger automatic Canadian [Salvage Title](/glossary/salvage-title/) branding, which is why pre-purchase inspection plus paint-thickness gauge check on body panels is non-negotiable on any JDM import.

### Can I drive a JDM car as a daily driver in Vancouver?

If it's a Japanese-spec version of a car sold worldwide (JDM Civic, Corolla, Pathfinder), yes, as long as you have a shop to look after it. For specialty JDM-only models (Skyline GT-R, Chaser, Acty kei truck, Honda Beat), daily-driver use is feasible but adds complexity: right-hand-drive ergonomics, parts sourcing through Japanese specialty importers, an independent shop relationship for service, and insurance considerations. Vancouver makes it easier than most Canadian cities, but the downsides don't go away, and most owners of these cars use them on weekends or as projects.
