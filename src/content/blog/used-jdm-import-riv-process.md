Canada is one of the most permissive [JDM](/glossary/jdm/) import markets in North America. Once a vehicle is fifteen years past its month of manufacture, Ottawa no longer regulates it under the Motor Vehicle Safety Act. In 2026 that means a JDM car built in, say, March 2011 can come in from March 2026, without meeting the Canadian Motor Vehicle Safety Standards and without going through the federal RIV program. The United States, by comparison, requires twenty-five years.

That last point trips up a lot of first-time importers. The [Registrar of Imported Vehicles](/glossary/riv-program/) (RIV) runs the federal compliance process for vehicles imported from the United States that are under 15 years old. A 15-year-old car from Japan never enters it. There's no RIV fee, no federal modification list and no federal inspection at Canadian Tire. What's left is customs, taxes and your province's own inspection and registration. Below we go through it step by step, with costs and the places importers usually go wrong.

## What is the 15-year rule?

Vehicles 15 years old or older, counted from the month and year of manufacture, are no longer regulated under the Motor Vehicle Safety Act because of their age. Transport Canada doesn't regulate their importation, so they don't have to meet current Canadian Motor Vehicle Safety Standards and they don't go through the [RIV Program](/glossary/riv-program/). They still have to meet provincial safety and licensing rules before they can be registered.

Below 15 years, the picture is very different. A vehicle under 15 has to have been built to Canadian or US safety standards, and the RIV program exists to bring US-market vehicles of that age into compliance. A JDM vehicle built for the Japanese domestic market generally can't be imported at all until it turns 15.

The age is calculated from the original date of manufacture, not the model year. A JDM vehicle with a January 2011 production date can be imported in January 2026. One with a December 2011 production date has to wait until December 2026. Check the production date before you buy. It's usually on the door-jamb sticker or the firewall plate, and your broker can confirm it from the Japanese export paperwork.

## Step 1: Customs entry at the port of arrival

The process starts with the Canada Border Services Agency. Most JDM cars land at the Port of Vancouver. Fewer go to East Coast ports such as Halifax, and some cars landed in Vancouver continue east by rail or truck. At the port, the importer or their customs broker completes the Vehicle Import Form (Form 1) with CBSA and shows proof of ownership: the original Japanese export certificate (with a translation), the bill of lading, and the bill of sale from the seller.

CBSA collects duty, excise tax and GST at this point:

- **Duty.** A car built in Japan can enter duty-free under the CPTPP trade agreement if you have a certification of origin. Without one, the standard 6.1% rate on passenger vehicles applies. CUSMA (the old NAFTA) has nothing to do with a Japanese-built car. Ask your broker which treatment they will claim and what paperwork they need from you.
- **Excise tax.** A $100 federal excise tax applies if the car has air conditioning.
- **GST.** 5% on the value for duty plus duty and excise.

Provincial sales tax is usually paid later, when you register the car. In Ontario you pay the 8% provincial part of the HST at ServiceOntario, so the full 13% isn't charged twice. In BC, PST on a vehicle imported from outside Canada starts at 7% for a passenger vehicle under $55,000 and rises with price; you pay it when you register through ICBC. Quebec collects QST when the SAAQ registers the car. Alberta has no provincial sales tax.

With the paperwork in order, CBSA usually clears the Form 1 quickly. The stamped Form 1 is what your province will ask to see, so keep the original safe. Clearing promptly also stops the port storage and demurrage charges that pile up while a car sits waiting.

## Step 2: No RIV fee, no federal modifications

For a 15-year-old import, this step is mostly about what you don't have to do. You don't pay the RIV fee, you don't need RIV's federal modification list (daytime running lights, child-seat tethers and the like are not required federally on a car this old), and there's no federal Form 2 inspection. Be wary of any broker or website that charges you an "RIV fee" for a 15-year-old car from Japan.

That doesn't mean the car is ready for the road. Your province's inspection will still check lights, brakes, tires, glass, steering and structure, and a car that has sat in storage often needs work to pass. Some owners add daytime running lights anyway, because they're useful and cheap.

Open recalls are worth checking, though the system doesn't force you to. Run the [VIN](/glossary/vin/) or chassis number past a same-brand Canadian dealer. Many JDM models were never sold here, so the dealer may have no record, but where a Canadian-market twin shares the part, the dealer can tell you whether a known safety campaign applies.

## Step 3: Provincial safety inspection

Each province has its own inspection for vehicles coming from outside it. The [Provincial Safety Inspection](/glossary/provincial-safety-inspection/) checks mechanical roadworthiness (brakes, suspension, steering, lights, tires, exhaust, structure). For a 15-year-old import it's the only safety inspection the car gets, so take it seriously.

Ontario requires a Safety Standards Certificate (SSC), valid for 36 days from issue, performed at a licensed Motor Vehicle Inspection Station. Cost typically runs $80 to $150 plus any required repairs. Alberta requires an Out-of-Province Inspection (OOPI) at a facility licensed under the province's Vehicle Inspection Program, typically $150 to $250 plus repairs. British Columbia requires a Designated Inspection Facility inspection for most JDM imports, typically $250 to $500 plus repairs. Quebec requires a SAAQ mechanical inspection for vehicles coming from outside the province. Other provinces apply variations of these models.

## Step 4: Provincial registration and insurance

Once the car passes, register it through the provincial office (ServiceOntario in Ontario, a registry agent in Alberta, an ICBC Autoplan broker in BC, the SAAQ in Quebec). Bring the stamped Form 1, the export certificate and translation, the bill of sale and the inspection certificate. This is also when you pay provincial sales tax. Registration fees run $70 to $150 depending on province, plus plates and any document fees.

Insurance gets sorted at the same time. On a right-hand-drive car or a specialty JDM model, confirm you can get coverage before you import, because some Canadian insurers won't write it.

## Total cost summary

Take a typical mid-value import: a $25,000 CAD landed-value vehicle (purchase price plus shipping and insurance) going to Ontario. Federal and provincial costs come to approximately:

- CBSA duty: $0 with a CPTPP certification of origin, or $1,525 at the 6.1% rate without one
- Air-conditioning excise tax: $100
- GST: about $1,255 (5% of value plus excise), or about $1,330 if duty applies
- Ontario's 8% provincial part of HST, paid at ServiceOntario: about $2,000
- RIV fee and federal inspection: none for a 15-year-old vehicle
- Provincial Safety Standards Certificate: $80 to $150 plus any required repairs
- Provincial registration and plates: $90 to $150
- Customs broker fee and insurance: varies

Total federal-and-provincial cost on top of the $25,000 landed-value vehicle: roughly $3,500 to $3,700 if the car enters duty-free, or about $5,100 to $5,300 if the 6.1% duty applies, before inspection repairs and broker fees. Almost all of it is tax.

## Common buyer mistakes

First-time importers make the same three mistakes. The first is underestimating the timeline. Japanese auction processing, the wait for a ship, the crossing itself and port handling take longer than most people expect, and spring and summer are busier. Plan for a couple of months from auction to plates.

The second is not checking the auction grade. Japanese auction houses use a grading system that captures pre-import damage history. A "clean" auction-bought JDM may carry a grade of 4 or 4.5 (minor cosmetic flaws), or it may carry R, RA or A, which flag accident damage or repair. None of those grades leads to a Canadian [Salvage Title](/glossary/salvage-title/) brand, because the damage happened in Japan, not through a Canadian insurance write-off. On any JDM import, get a pre-purchase inspection and have the body panels checked with a paint-thickness gauge.

The third is importing before you know you can insure it. Some Canadian insurers decline right-hand-drive vehicles entirely. Some decline specialty JDM models (Skyline GT-R, certain kei vehicles). Others add surcharges big enough to change what the car really costs you. Get a quote from your insurer before you commit.

## Common questions

### What is the 15-year rule for importing cars to Canada?

Once a vehicle is 15 years old, counted from its month and year of manufacture, it's no longer regulated under the federal Motor Vehicle Safety Act. It can be imported without meeting current Canadian Motor Vehicle Safety Standards and without going through the [RIV Program](/glossary/riv-program/), though it still has to pass your province's inspection and registration. Vehicles from outside the US that are under 15 generally can't be imported at all. That 15-year line, against the US 25-year rule, is why Canada is such an easy [JDM](/glossary/jdm/) market.

### How much does the RIV process cost?

For a 15-year-old car from Japan, nothing, because the RIV process doesn't apply. RIV handles vehicles under 15 years old coming from the United States. Your real costs are customs and tax: possible 6.1% duty (zero if the car qualifies under CPTPP and you have a certification of origin), a $100 excise tax if it has air conditioning, 5% GST at the border, and provincial sales tax when you register. Add a provincial safety inspection ($80 to $500 depending on province, plus repairs), registration and plates, and your broker's fee.

### Where do I do the RIV inspection?

You don't, for a 15-year-old JDM import. The federal RIV inspection at Canadian Tire is for vehicles going through the RIV program, which means US-market vehicles under 15 years old. Your JDM car needs your province's inspection instead: an Ontario Safety Standards Certificate at a licensed Motor Vehicle Inspection Station, an Alberta Out-of-Province Inspection at a licensed facility, a BC Designated Inspection Facility inspection, or a SAAQ mechanical inspection in Quebec.

### What modifications does Canada require on JDM imports?

At the federal level, none for a vehicle 15 years or older. It's exempt from the Canadian Motor Vehicle Safety Standards, so items like daytime running lights aren't required by Ottawa. The car still has to pass your province's safety inspection, which can mean repairs to brakes, tires, lights, glass or rust before it passes. Many owners add daytime running lights anyway because they're cheap and useful.

### How long does customs clearance take?

With the paperwork in order (Form 1, original Japanese export certificate with translation, bill of lading, proof of purchase), CBSA usually clears a vehicle quickly, often the same day. Import brokers do this all the time. Most of the total wait is elsewhere: auction processing and shipping before the car arrives, port handling after it lands, and booking the provincial inspection and any repairs before registration. Spring and summer are the busiest seasons, particularly at the Port of Vancouver.
