EV and PHEV adoption in Canada kept climbing through Q2 2026, as it has for roughly five years. Federal money has been patchy: the iZEV rebate ran out of money and closed in January 2025, and its replacement only started paying in February 2026. Provincial help has shrunk too. Better winter range on second-generation EVs has helped more. Japanese brands still offer fewer EVs here than Korean, German or American makers. A few Japanese models do have a real foothold, though, and used Toyota hybrids have held their value unusually well.

This Q2 2026 update covers rebates, the Japanese models on sale, winter range, and how used prices are moving.

## The 2026 federal EV rebate: EVAP replaces iZEV

The federal Incentives for Zero-Emission Vehicles programme (iZEV) ran out of money and closed in January 2025. For about a year there was no federal rebate at all. On February 16, 2026, Transport Canada started the Electric Vehicle Affordability Program (EVAP).

EVAP pays up to $5,000 on a new battery [electric vehicle](/glossary/ev/) or fuel cell vehicle and up to $2,500 on a new [PHEV](/glossary/phev/). It covers purchases and leases. The final transaction value has to be $50,000 or less, except for EVs built in Canada, which have no price cap. The amounts step down each year, and the program runs to March 31, 2031. It applies to new vehicles only.

Provincial help is thinner than it was. BC paused its CleanBC Go Electric passenger-vehicle rebate in May 2025 and ended it in November 2025. Quebec's Roulez vert still pays in 2026, up to $2,000 on a new BEV and up to $1,000 on a new PHEV, but the program ends on December 31, 2026. A Quebec buyer of a qualifying new BEV can therefore still combine up to $5,000 federal and $2,000 provincial in 2026. Alberta and Ontario have no provincial rebate; Ontario cancelled its programme in 2018.

Used buyers get nothing from EVAP, just as they got nothing from iZEV. Quebec's Roulez vert also offers a smaller rebate on eligible used EVs through 2026, with its own eligibility rules.

## Japanese-brand EV/PHEV lineup in 2026

These are the EVs and PHEVs the Japanese brands we cover sell in Canada:

**Battery Electric Vehicles (BEVs):**
- Nissan Leaf (since 2011; the third generation arrived for 2026)
- Nissan Ariya (introduced 2022)
- Toyota bZ4X
- Lexus RZ
- Subaru Solterra (mechanically related to bZ4X)
- Mazda MX-30 (sold in Canada from 2022 to the 2024 model year, now on the used market only)

**Plug-in Hybrid Electric Vehicles (PHEVs):**
- Toyota Prius Prime
- Toyota RAV4 Prime
- Lexus NX 450h+
- Lexus RX 450h+ (current generation)
- Mitsubishi Outlander PHEV (on sale with AWD longer than almost any other PHEV here)

**Conventional hybrids (no plug, not eligible for federal EV incentives):**
- Toyota Camry Hybrid, Corolla Hybrid, Prius (regular hybrid), RAV4 Hybrid, Highlander Hybrid, Sienna (hybrid-only), Avalon Hybrid (discontinued)
- Lexus ES 300h, NX 350h, RX 350h (RX 450h before 2023), UX 250h, LS 500h, LC 500h
- Honda CR-V Hybrid, Accord Hybrid, Civic Hybrid (returned for 2025)

Honda's battery-electric models so far come from its partnership with General Motors on the Ultium platform: the Honda Prologue, still on sale, and the Acura ZDX, built only as a 2024 model before Acura cancelled it in 2025. Honda has not yet launched a Honda-engineered BEV in the Canadian market. Apart from the Lexus RZ, the luxury brands (Acura, Infiniti, Lexus) have little or nothing electric on sale yet.

The Mitsubishi Outlander PHEV is worth singling out. It's one of the only plug-in SUVs with full AWD designed for Canadian winters, and its twin-motor [S-AWC](/glossary/s-awc/) drivetrain handles changing grip well. Used 2018-2022 examples in Calgary, Vancouver, and Toronto move quickly when listed at competitive pricing.

## Cold-weather range performance: Q2 2026 update

Second-generation EVs lose less range in the cold than the first-generation cars sold through 2022, but they still lose plenty. Range loss at -20°C against the NRCan rating:

- First-generation Nissan Leaf (2011-2017): 30 to 45 percent range loss in Canadian winter conditions
- Second-generation Nissan Leaf (2018+): 25 to 40 percent range loss
- Nissan Ariya: 22 to 35 percent range loss
- Toyota bZ4X / Lexus RZ / Subaru Solterra: 25 to 40 percent range loss

That's physics, and no current battery chemistry escapes it. Lithium-ion cells deliver less in the cold, heating the cabin eats battery energy, and tires roll harder on cold pavement. Battery thermal management and preconditioning while plugged in win some of it back. Heated seats and a heated wheel use less energy than blasting the cabin heater.

Plan on 25 to 40 percent less range than the rating in winter. A Nissan Leaf rated at 240 km combined will reliably deliver 145 to 180 km in Calgary or Edmonton January conditions. With today's batteries, you really need a Level-2 charger at home to live with an EV here day to day.

## Used hybrid market dynamics

Used Toyota hybrids hold their value better than the gas versions. The [e-CVT](/glossary/e-cvt/) is a big reason. Toronto and Montreal taxi fleets routinely run original hybrid transmissions past 500,000 km, and used buyers have noticed.

At three years old, a Camry Hybrid keeps noticeably more of its original price than a gas Camry, and the RAV4 Hybrid holds value about as well as the gas RAV4, which is already one of the best in the segment. You pay $2,000 to $3,500 more for the hybrid new, and you get most of it back at resale.

The Honda [i-MMD](/glossary/i-mmd/) hybrid system on the CR-V Hybrid and Accord Hybrid works differently, but it has proven just as durable over its shorter Canadian history (since 2020). Used CR-V Hybrid pricing typically runs $1,500 to $3,000 above the comparable gas trim and the value retention has tracked similarly to Toyota Hybrid equivalents.

PHEVs behave differently. The RAV4 Prime, NX 450h+ and Outlander PHEV all cost noticeably more new than their gas versions, but they depreciate faster, partly because the federal rebate the first owner got never passes to the next one. A 2022 RAV4 Prime that was new-purchased at $52,000 (after the iZEV rebate of the time) typically lists used at $36,000 to $42,000 in 2026. That's a big drop, and good news for the second owner.

## What is Q3 2026 likely to look like?

Watch two things through the rest of 2026. The first is incentives. EVAP is funded to March 31, 2031, but its amounts step down every year, and Quebec's Roulez vert ends on December 31, 2026, so a buyer of a new EV in Quebec gets more this year than next. The second is new Japanese EVs. Honda's Prologue still comes from General Motors, a Honda-engineered BEV has yet to reach Canada, and Toyota has signalled additional EV launches beyond bZ4X. Whether either reshapes the Canadian Japanese-brand EV market in 2026 remains to be seen.

We expect the used market to keep doing what it's been doing. Toyota and Honda hybrids will hold value. PHEVs will keep dropping as new cars bought with incentives come off their first owners. BEV prices will keep reacting to worries about battery wear and winter range. Our used picks for 2026 haven't changed: a Toyota hybrid (RAV4 Hybrid, Camry Hybrid, Highlander Hybrid) or a Mitsubishi Outlander PHEV.

## Common questions

### What federal EV rebate is available in Canada in 2026?

The Electric Vehicle Affordability Program (EVAP), which started on February 16, 2026. It replaced iZEV, which ran out of money and closed in January 2025. EVAP pays up to $5,000 on a new battery [electric vehicle](/glossary/ev/) or fuel cell vehicle and up to $2,500 on a new [PHEV](/glossary/phev/), on purchases or leases with a final transaction value of $50,000 or less (no cap for Canadian-built EVs). Amounts step down each year, and the program runs to March 31, 2031. Used vehicles don't qualify. Check current rules on Transport Canada's EVAP page before you buy.

### Which Japanese EVs are sold in Canada?

Within the japanauto.ca brand whitelist (Toyota, Honda, Nissan, Mazda, Subaru, Lexus, Acura, Infiniti, Mitsubishi), the current Battery Electric lineup includes Nissan Leaf, Nissan Ariya, Toyota bZ4X, Lexus RZ, Subaru Solterra (related to bZ4X), and Mazda MX-30 with limited availability. PHEV options include Toyota Prius Prime, Toyota RAV4 Prime, Lexus NX 450h+, Lexus RX 450h+, and Mitsubishi Outlander PHEV. Honda has not yet launched a Honda-engineered BEV in Canada; the Honda Prologue and the discontinued Acura ZDX (2024 only) use the General Motors Ultium platform. The Mazda MX-30 BEV was sold here through the 2024 model year.

### Are hybrids still worth it in 2026?

Yes, and more so the more city kilometres you drive. Toyota Hybrid Synergy Drive vehicles ([e-CVT](/glossary/e-cvt/)-based) have demonstrated 500,000+ km service life in taxi fleet use and unusually strong used-market resale. Honda [i-MMD](/glossary/i-mmd/) on CR-V Hybrid and Accord Hybrid is similarly competent. The hybrid premium over gas equivalents typically pays back through fuel savings within 80,000 to 120,000 km of metropolitan driving. Note that conventional hybrids do not qualify for the federal EVAP incentive (only new [PHEVs](/glossary/phev/), [BEVs](/glossary/ev/) and fuel cell vehicles do). If you can't charge at home, a hybrid is still the sensible way to electrify.

### How are used EV prices trending in Canada?

Used EV prices in Canada have softened through 2024-2026, as rebate-discounted new cars and fast-changing new models pulled used values down. A 2022 Nissan Leaf typically lists used in Toronto or Vancouver at $18,000 to $24,000 in 2026 versus the original new-MSRP of approximately $35,000 to $42,000 (after the iZEV rebate of the time). PHEV used pricing has followed similar steep depreciation patterns. Used Toyota and Honda conventional hybrids have held value better than EVs and PHEVs, and better than their own gas versions. If you can live with winter range and the state of public charging, that depreciation makes a used EV or PHEV a good deal.

### What's the difference between EVAP and provincial rebates?

EVAP is the federal program, available on qualifying new vehicles anywhere in Canada: up to $5,000 on a BEV or fuel cell vehicle and up to $2,500 on a PHEV, with a $50,000 transaction-value cap that doesn't apply to Canadian-built EVs. Provincial rebates are separate and run by each province. In 2026 only Quebec's Roulez vert is still paying (up to $2,000 on a new BEV, up to $1,000 on a new PHEV, ending December 31, 2026), and it can be combined with EVAP. BC's CleanBC Go Electric passenger rebate was paused in May 2025 and ended in November 2025. Alberta and Ontario offer no provincial EV rebate. Used vehicles don't qualify for EVAP; Roulez vert has a separate, smaller rebate for eligible used EVs.
